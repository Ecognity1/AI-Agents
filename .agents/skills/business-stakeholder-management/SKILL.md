# Stakeholder Management Skill

## Purpose

This skill defines principles, standards, and best practices for stakeholder identification, analysis, engagement, communication, expectation management, and decision alignment.

Effective stakeholder management helps ensure that:

- Relevant perspectives are represented.
- Decision authority is understood.
- Expectations are aligned.
- Conflicts are identified early.
- Decisions are made by appropriate stakeholders.
- Communication is appropriate and timely.
- Changes are understood by affected parties.

This skill is:

- Domain-neutral
- Technology-neutral
- Solution-neutral
- Industry-neutral

It should be used as organizational knowledge rather than as a project workflow.

---

## Objectives

Stakeholder management should help:

- Identify relevant stakeholders.
- Understand stakeholder interests and expectations.
- Understand influence and decision authority.
- Clarify roles and responsibilities.
- Identify conflicting priorities.
- Establish appropriate engagement approaches.
- Support effective decision-making.
- Maintain alignment throughout change.
- Reduce communication gaps.
- Improve stakeholder confidence.
- Ensure affected groups are appropriately represented.

---

## Definitions

### Stakeholder

An individual, group, organization, or external party that:

- Has an interest in an initiative.
- Can influence an outcome.
- Makes or contributes to decisions.
- Is affected by an outcome.
- Provides required information or resources.

### Stakeholder Identification

The process of determining who has relevant interests, responsibilities, influence, knowledge, or impact.

### Stakeholder Analysis

The assessment of stakeholder characteristics such as:

- Interest
- Influence
- Impact
- Authority
- Expectations
- Concerns
- Information needs

### Stakeholder Engagement

The intentional involvement of stakeholders in communication, consultation, collaboration, decision-making, or validation.

### Decision Authority

The recognized authority to approve, reject, prioritize, or make a particular decision.

### Stakeholder Expectation

An outcome, behavior, level of involvement, or result expected by a stakeholder.

### Stakeholder Conflict

A situation where stakeholder goals, priorities, requirements, expectations, or constraints are incompatible or perceived to be incompatible.

## Fundamental Principles

### Identify Stakeholders Early

Stakeholders should be identified as early as reasonably possible.

Late stakeholder discovery can cause:

- Missing requirements
- Conflicting expectations
- Rework
- Delayed decisions
- Scope changes
- Adoption problems

Stakeholder identification should continue as understanding evolves.

---

### Identify Stakeholders by Role, Not Only by Name

Stakeholder analysis should focus primarily on responsibilities and interests.

Examples include:

- Decision Makers
- Sponsors
- Business Owners
- Subject Matter Experts
- Operational Stakeholders
- Governance Stakeholders
- Risk Stakeholders
- Users or Consumers
- Support Functions
- External Parties

Named individuals may change.

Roles and responsibilities generally provide more durable context.

---

### Understand Impact

Different stakeholders may experience different effects.

Consider:

- Direct impact
- Indirect impact
- Operational impact
- Financial impact
- Regulatory impact
- Organizational impact
- Process impact

Stakeholders experiencing significant impact should receive appropriate consideration even when their formal authority is limited.

---

### Understand Influence

Stakeholders may influence:

- Funding
- Priorities
- Requirements
- Governance
- Risk acceptance
- Scope
- Adoption
- Decisions

Formal authority and practical influence are not always the same.

Both should be considered.

---

### Understand Decision Authority

Clearly identify who has authority to decide significant matters.

Examples include decisions about:

- Scope
- Priority
- Funding
- Risk acceptance
- Requirements
- Compliance
- Schedule
- Policy
- Exceptions

Avoid assuming consensus when formal approval is required.

---

## Stakeholder Identification

Consider stakeholders from multiple perspectives.

### Strategic

Those responsible for:

- Organizational objectives
- Strategy
- Investment
- Business outcomes

### Operational

Those responsible for:

- Day-to-day activities
- Processes
- Service delivery
- Support

### Governance

Those responsible for:

- Policies
- Standards
- Compliance
- Risk
- Audit
- Oversight

### Subject Matter

Those possessing specialized knowledge required for informed decisions.

### Affected Groups

Those whose responsibilities, experience, processes, or outcomes may change.

### External Stakeholders

Where relevant, consider:

- Customers
- Partners
- Suppliers
- Regulators
- Contractual parties
- Industry bodies

Do not assume stakeholder groups are limited to organizational boundaries.

---

## Stakeholder Analysis

Relevant stakeholder characteristics may include:

| Attribute | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Role | Understand responsibility |
| Interest | Understand level of concern |
| Influence | Understand ability to affect outcomes |
| Impact | Understand degree of change experienced |
| Authority | Understand decision rights |
| Expectations | Understand desired outcomes |
| Concerns | Identify potential issues |
| Expertise | Identify knowledge contribution |
| Engagement Need | Determine appropriate involvement |

Only collect information that provides practical decision or engagement value.

---

## Influence and Interest

A common analysis technique considers stakeholder influence and interest.

### High Influence / High Interest

Generally requires active engagement and close alignment.

### High Influence / Low Interest

Maintain appropriate communication and ensure important decisions are visible.

### Low Influence / High Interest

Provide relevant information and opportunities for feedback.

### Low Influence / Low Interest

Monitor and communicate when appropriate.

This model is guidance rather than a rigid classification system.

---

## Impact and Influence

Influence alone should not determine stakeholder importance.

A stakeholder may have:

- Low authority
- Low organizational influence
- High impact

Such stakeholders may still provide critical information about:

- Practical consequences
- Usability
- Operational risks
- Adoption
- Process impacts

Consider both **influence** and **impact**.

---

## Roles and Responsibilities

Important stakeholder responsibilities should be clear.

Where useful, responsibility models such as RACI may be used.

### Responsible

Performs or coordinates the work.

### Accountable

Owns the final outcome or decision.

### Consulted

Provides expertise or input.

### Informed

Requires awareness of decisions or outcomes.

Avoid assigning multiple accountable parties to a single decision without clear governance.

---

## Decision Rights

Important decisions should have identifiable owners.

A useful decision record may include:

| Decision | Decision Owner | Contributors | Status | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Decision ownership should be established before significant disagreements arise.

---

## Stakeholder Expectations

Expectations should be identified and validated.

Consider expectations relating to:

- Outcomes
- Scope
- Quality
- Timing
- Cost
- Risk
- Participation
- Communication
- Decision authority

Unrealistic or conflicting expectations should be addressed explicitly.

Do not allow assumptions about stakeholder expectations to become implicit commitments.

---

## Stakeholder Needs vs. Requirements

Stakeholder requests should not automatically become requirements.

A stakeholder may request a specific solution.

The underlying need should first be understood.

For example:

Stakeholder request:

> We need a particular tool.

Underlying need may be:

> We need a reliable way to reduce manual processing.

Understanding the need allows alternatives to be evaluated.

---

## Stakeholder Engagement

Engagement should be proportional to:

- Influence
- Impact
- Expertise
- Decision authority
- Risk
- Complexity
- Information needs

Possible engagement approaches include:

- Inform
- Consult
- Collaborate
- Validate
- Approve
- Decide

Not every stakeholder needs the same level of involvement.

---

## Communication Principles

Communication should be:

- Clear
- Relevant
- Timely
- Audience-appropriate
- Actionable where necessary
- Consistent

Avoid sending identical levels of detail to every stakeholder.

Different audiences may require different perspectives.

---

## Communication Content

Depending on stakeholder needs, communication may include:

- Objectives
- Decisions
- Risks
- Dependencies
- Changes
- Progress
- Open Questions
- Actions
- Required Approvals

Communication should emphasize information relevant to the recipient.

---

## Communication Frequency

Communication frequency should reflect:

- Rate of change
- Decision urgency
- Risk
- Stakeholder impact
- Governance expectations

Avoid both:

- Under-communication
- Excessive communication without decision value

---

## Stakeholder Feedback

Feedback should be:

- Captured accurately
- Evaluated objectively
- Connected to relevant decisions or requirements
- Resolved where necessary

Feedback should not automatically be accepted as a requirement.

Evaluate it against:

- Business objectives
- Scope
- Value
- Risk
- Constraints
- Other stakeholder needs

---

## Conflict Management

Stakeholder conflict is normal.

Conflicts may involve:

- Priorities
- Scope
- Cost
- Risk
- Requirements
- Policies
- Resources
- Timelines
- Desired outcomes

Conflicts should be made visible rather than hidden.

---

## Conflict Resolution Principles

When stakeholder expectations conflict:

1. Identify the actual disagreement.
2. Separate positions from underlying interests.
3. Identify relevant business objectives.
4. Understand constraints.
5. Evaluate impact.
6. Identify decision authority.
7. Compare reasonable alternatives.
8. Record the resulting decision and rationale.

Do not resolve significant stakeholder conflicts by silently selecting one position.

---

## Consensus

Consensus can be valuable but is not always necessary or possible.

Do not confuse:

**Consultation**

with

**Approval**

or:

**Agreement**

with

**Decision Authority**

Where consensus cannot be achieved, the authorized decision-maker should make the decision using available evidence.

---

## Stakeholder Prioritization

When stakeholder needs compete, consider:

- Business objectives
- Strategic alignment
- Impact
- Risk
- Regulatory obligations
- Organizational policies
- Customer or user value
- Decision authority

Do not prioritize requirements solely based on stakeholder seniority.

---

## Stakeholder Changes

Stakeholders may change over time.

Changes may include:

- New decision-makers
- Organizational restructuring
- Responsibility changes
- New external parties
- Changed priorities

Stakeholder analysis should therefore be reviewed when significant change occurs.

---

## Sensitive Stakeholder Information

Stakeholder documentation should contain only information necessary for effective engagement and governance.

Avoid unnecessary recording of:

- Personal judgments
- Informal opinions
- Sensitive personal information
- Unsupported characterizations

Describe observable roles, responsibilities, concerns, and decision needs objectively.

---

## Decision Guidelines

When determining whether a stakeholder should be involved, consider:

1. Are they affected by the outcome?
2. Do they have relevant expertise?
3. Do they own a requirement or objective?
4. Do they control required resources?
5. Do they have decision authority?
6. Do they govern risk or compliance?
7. Could their absence create significant misunderstanding or rework?

If several apply, involvement is likely important.

---

## Best Practices

- Identify stakeholders early.
- Reassess stakeholders when circumstances change.
- Understand both influence and impact.
- Clarify decision authority.
- Separate stakeholder requests from underlying needs.
- Tailor communication to the audience.
- Make conflicts visible.
- Record significant decisions.
- Validate assumptions about stakeholder expectations.
- Include affected stakeholders appropriately.
- Avoid unnecessary communication overhead.
- Maintain objective stakeholder documentation.
- Align engagement intensity with risk and impact.

---

## Quality Considerations

Effective stakeholder management should provide:

### Representation

Relevant perspectives are appropriately represented.

### Clarity

Roles and decision responsibilities are understood.

### Alignment

Stakeholder expectations are reasonably aligned with objectives.

### Transparency

Important decisions and conflicts are visible.

### Responsiveness

Important concerns receive appropriate attention.

### Efficiency

Stakeholder involvement is proportional to value and need.

---

## Trade-offs

Stakeholder management often requires balancing:

- Broad participation vs. decision speed
- Consensus vs. accountability
- Detail vs. communication efficiency
- Stakeholder preference vs. organizational priorities
- Local optimization vs. overall value
- Transparency vs. information sensitivity

These trade-offs should be handled deliberately.

---

## Common Mistakes

Avoid:

- Identifying only senior stakeholders.
- Ignoring people significantly affected by change.
- Assuming authority based on job title alone.
- Treating all stakeholders equally.
- Treating every stakeholder request as a requirement.
- Assuming silence means agreement.
- Confusing consultation with approval.
- Allowing unclear decision ownership.
- Avoiding stakeholder conflicts.
- Over-communicating irrelevant detail.
- Under-communicating significant changes.
- Ignoring external stakeholders.
- Failing to reassess stakeholder relationships after major changes.
- Recording subjective or unnecessary personal judgments.

---

## Validation Checklist

Before considering stakeholder analysis sufficiently complete, verify:

- [ ] Relevant stakeholder groups have been identified.
- [ ] Business ownership is understood.
- [ ] Decision authority is clear for significant decisions.
- [ ] Stakeholder influence has been considered.
- [ ] Stakeholder impact has been considered.
- [ ] Relevant subject matter expertise is represented.
- [ ] Governance and risk stakeholders are identified where relevant.
- [ ] External stakeholders are considered where relevant.
- [ ] Stakeholder expectations are understood where necessary.
- [ ] Conflicting expectations are visible.
- [ ] Roles and responsibilities are sufficiently clear.
- [ ] Engagement approaches are appropriate.
- [ ] Communication needs are understood.
- [ ] Significant decisions have identifiable owners.
- [ ] Stakeholder requests have been distinguished from underlying needs.
- [ ] Stakeholder information is documented objectively.
- [ ] Stakeholder analysis can be updated when circumstances change.
